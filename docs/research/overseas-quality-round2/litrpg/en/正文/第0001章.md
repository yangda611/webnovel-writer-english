# 第0001章：What a Hand Can Hold

The registrar had put a chair on top of my cargo. She stood on it to reach a nail, carrying a framed portrait of the mayor under one arm.

“The case goes first,” I said.

“The case can swim.”

“Then you hired the wrong porter.”

Esme looked down at me. Her spectacles had slipped almost to the end of her nose. On other mornings she made me wipe my boots before entering the records office; today the canal was coming through the floorboards, and she was saving a portrait.

“He's covering the emergency key,” she said. She lifted the frame off its nail. Behind it hung a red cord. “Give me your knife.”

I gave her the knife.

The office clock showed seven twenty. My contract gave me forty minutes to carry the residency register across the canal to the High Landing records desk. The clerk there had to check the copper seam and sign the receipt before the twelve silver became payable. Nine would replace the wheel on my leased handcart. The other three would buy Dad a week of coal for the room where the evacuation people had put him. I had two silver left in my pocket, and neither of us owned a winter coat worth burning.

Esme cut the cord and pocketed the key. Then she climbed down, using my shoulder for the last step.

“Thirty-four kilos, including the case,” she said. “I weighed it yesterday.”

That left six kilos within my skill's limit. Not six kilos of luggage I could add: six kilos before the skill refused the entire load. Apprentices learned the difference by dropping things.

My cart waited outside, its damaged wheel packed with wedges. I tested the case's handles. Both held. The copper seam around the lid was unbroken.

“Why paper?” I asked. “You people send my late fees through the civic mesh quickly enough.”

“The inland copy updates on Fridays.”

“Today is Tuesday.”

“Yes.” She took a waterproof sleeve from her desk. “These establish who's entitled to an evacuation room. A late fee can wait until Friday.”

Dad's address was in there somewhere: one room above the closed chandlery, a stove he could reach from his chair. The evacuation clerk had told him to bring proof. He had brought an envelope full of photographs.

Outside, something scraped for longer than a wave should scrape.

Esme glanced toward the courtyard. “The shutter.”

She reached the doorway before I did. The storm shutter had pulled free of its upper brackets. One corner struck the flagstones, and the other came down across her boot. She fell sideways. The shutter settled with its broad face against the opposite wall, making a low metal tent over her leg.

I caught its upper edge. Cold water ran into my sleeve.

“Don't pull,” she said. “The boot's caught on a bolt.”

I could get the shutter up an inch. I couldn't hold it there and turn it. My thumb found the old maker's plate: thirty-eight kilos. It was loose from the building now; I could see daylight through both torn brackets.

I called the one skill my license had earned me.

> Lift I — 2 Focus. One nonliving object, up to 40 kg. Weight suspended for 10 seconds while touched. Momentum unchanged. Recovery: 1 Focus per uninterrupted 5 minutes seated; maximum 8.

The rules were mine to see. Esme could see only my hand sliding on the wet edge.

“Ten seconds,” I told her. “Get the boot off.”

Focus fell from eight to six. The shutter ceased to bear down on my wrist. It still tried to sail sideways in the wind. I braced my hip against it and kept my right hand on the metal.

Esme pulled at her laces.

“Cut them.”

“Your knife's in my—”

“Pocket. Yes.”

She found it. I counted aloud, because when people had ten seconds they tended to spend the first four asking how many remained.

At seven, she pulled her foot free. At eight, she crawled clear. I lowered the shutter toward an empty patch of stones, using my left hand to keep its corner away from her. A peeled strip of metal cut through my glove.

At ten, its weight returned. It hit the stones. I let go, and the ringing went through my teeth.

For a moment neither of us spoke. Then Esme held up my knife, still open.

“I can have those laces replaced,” she said.

Blood ran into my cuff. “Put it on my invoice.”

A small line opened at the edge of my sight.

> Licensed lifts completed: 3/3. Specialization available.

The other two had been dull jobs, each held for the full ten seconds: a broken crate at the flour depot, a stove beneath a jammed hoist. I'd spent three weeks hoping for a third qualifying lift. Now I wished I'd earned it on something with handles.

Esme tied her handkerchief around my palm. Her left ankle had begun to swell. She put her bare foot down, tried a little weight, and immediately took it off again.

“Can you sit on the cart?” I asked.

“With the register?”

“You aren't part of it. The skill wouldn't count you as cargo.”

“I meant the wheel.”

So did I, once I looked at it.

We dragged the case to the front steps. The cart's wedges had washed out. Its wheel leaned against the axle, useless, and the public staircase across the street was already under brown water.

The remaining route was the old quay service walk. A section of railing had come away, leaving a narrow run above the canal. There was room for one person and no place to set a case down. Esme could manage it with a hand on the wall. She could not help me carry thirty-four kilos along it.

My specialization waited. There were two entries.

> Economy: Lift costs 1 Focus; duration remains 10 seconds.
>
> Long Carry: Lift lasts 20 seconds; cost increases to 3 Focus.
>
> Choose one. Maximum mass and contact requirement remain unchanged.

Esme read them aloud after I told her. She made Economy sound like a sensible insurance policy. Six lifts before I needed to rest, if I chose it. Two, if I chose Long Carry. Neither choice replenished what I had spent.

“We can wait for a boat,” she said.

“Will one stop?”

“I can't promise that.”

We could also leave the case, climb to the roof, and keep our names attached to our living bodies. Someone inland would eventually sort out the rooms. Dad might spend the interval on a barracks floor, but he would not be the only one.

I looked along the service walk. The crossing itself would take less than twenty seconds. I had used it delivering flour. Ten seconds might have got me across empty-handed, if I ran, and I could not run with the case's inertia tugging at my arm.

“I want the long one,” I said.

“Then tell me before you use the last of it.”

That sounded less like permission than the beginning of an arrangement. I selected Long Carry. The entry settled into the place Lift I had occupied. No light, no warmth, no mending of my hand. Only a changed line in a contract my body had to honor.

At seven twenty-seven, Esme went first. She kept one hand against the brickwork and hopped when she had to. From the far end she showed me both palms: clear.

I took the case in my right hand and spent three Focus. Three remained.

The case rose easily. Turning it was harder. I had to stop its swing before stepping out, because thirty-four kilos moving sideways could still take me into the canal.

At the tenth second, I reached the missing railing. My injured hand scraped the wall. The old skill would have ended there. This one held, and I moved my back foot past the gap.

“Keep coming,” Esme said.

I wished she wouldn't look at my hand.

The far stones were dry enough to grip. I reached them at eighteen and put the case down with my fingers still through its handle. At twenty, its weight returned. It stayed where I had placed it.

Esme sat beside it. I unwound the wet end of her handkerchief and tied it tighter. My palm hurt most when I straightened the fingers. That was going to matter on the next job, if there was a next job.

The ferry bell rang beyond the warehouse roof. Two short strokes: the loading window had changed.

We reached the ferry's embarkation quay at seven twenty-nine. Its board now read LAST CROSSING: 7:38. The gangway across the damaged berth had a new sign beneath it.

PASSENGERS ONLY. NO CARTS. NO SEALED FREIGHT.

Esme leaned on my good shoulder. The case stood between my boots, sealed and worth twelve silver, with half our neighborhood shut inside.
