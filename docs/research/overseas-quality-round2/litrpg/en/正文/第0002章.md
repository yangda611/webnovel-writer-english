# 第0002章：The Seal on the Case

The ferryman took one look at the case and pointed at his sign.

“The deck will carry it,” I said.

“The gangway won't take its wheels, and I have no freight crew left.”

“No wheels.” I showed him the handles.

“Then carry it on the seven-forty boat.”

“Your board says seven thirty-eight.”

“It did.” He pulled a strip from his pocket and struck out the last two digits. “Seven forty. The other landing's clearing a bed.”

Two minutes bought by someone else's trouble. I watched him write them as though they belonged to me.

It was still seven twenty-nine. Three Focus remained. My palm was cut under Esme's handkerchief, and her left ankle was swelling above the boot she couldn't put on. Neither problem had improved during the walk.

Beyond the ferryman, passengers crossed on a gangway made from two narrow planks. The berth's proper bridge had broken at its middle hinge. A porter could take a case along those planks, but a dropped load would pull the person behind it into the water. The ferryman wasn't merely being difficult. I found that inconvenient.

Esme asked for a chair. He gave her a fish crate.

“I need one too,” I said.

He looked at me, then at the blood on my cuff, and brought a second crate.

I sat at seven thirty. Five uninterrupted minutes would restore one Focus. Walking, working, or getting up would restart that interval. At four, I'd be able to make one Long Carry and have one left. Another five minutes would make five, still short of the six needed for two. The ferry would leave before I could earn six.

On the mesh's small clock, the minute changed.

“Don't let me stand,” I told Esme.

“I've been trying that for years.”

She took her handkerchief's loose tail between two fingers. I let her adjust it. Sitting was not healing. I could still feel the cut open when my thumb moved, and no line in my license promised to close it.

Behind us, a man argued over a pump bearing.

I knew bearings from invoices, usually covered in grease and accompanied by words the customer didn't want copied. This one sat in a sling beside the ferry office, a steel ring almost as wide as my chest. A paper tag gave its weight as thirty-nine kilos.

“Clean-water pump,” the man said. “West shelter. The truck can't reach the inland bank. This boat can.”

The ferryman folded his arms. “I've a driver over there to receive it. I haven't anybody here who can walk that thing across.”

“You,” the man said to me.

“Sitting.”

“You're licensed.”

“Also sitting.”

He stopped above my crate, breathing hard. His coat carried the yellow patch of the shelter kitchen. I had delivered flour there last winter. They fed people before asking which evacuation list they were on.

“They've water until noon,” he said. “After that, somebody boils canal water for three hundred people. Can your class move it?”

“Once.”

He glanced at my case. “Ah.”

Esme drew it closer with her good foot.

Nobody told me the bearing mattered more than the register. The man tapped the yellow patch on his coat. “My wife spent two weeks proving her residency after the fire,” he said. “Those books got her a room. We need the pump running by noon.” Esme shifted her bad ankle. “I staffed a shelter when its well pump failed. I know how fast a queue for clean water grows.”

“Can you open it?” he asked her.

“Yes.”

“Then we can carry the books.”

Esme stared at the copper seam. I watched the clock. Seven thirty-two.

“The seal certifies the whole collection,” she said. “Broken, it's four bundles anyone could lose, swap, or read.”

“They're already reading each other's letters over there.” He nodded toward the queue. A woman held an envelope open for a man whose hands shook. “We can find four people you know.”

“And I lose the delivery fee,” I said.

He looked away from me. That was worse than an argument.

I could still carry the intact case. The bearing would stay behind, and the shelter would ration water while another route was found. Or we could split the books and trust four tired neighbors with addresses, debts, old names, and the proof that Dad belonged in a room with a stove. It wasn't just money I would put at risk by opening it.

Esme said, “If we break it, I record every handover. You don't get to choose people just because they're nearest.”

“You'd authorize it?”

“I would authorize the opening. The delivery contract still requires a sealed case. The municipal exception fee is two silver, paid before a witness. We cannot waive it here.”

“I've two.”

“I know.”

She did know. She had counted them with me outside the wheelwright's yesterday, while explaining that a requisition was not the same thing as a payment.

At seven thirty-three, I asked the ferryman to witness. I stayed seated. Esme used her red key to open the outer lock; the ferryman clipped the copper seam. I put my two silver into his fee tin. He wrote my name and hers on the exception form, and gave me a receipt I had nowhere dry to put.

The case itself weighed eight kilos. Its books weighed twenty-six. Esme sorted them into four bundles: seven, seven, six, six. She used the records office's waterproof sleeves and cord from the ferryman. No book left her reach until she had read its number and the carrier's name aloud.

Mrs. Hobb, who had once refused to pay me for delivering a cracked sink, took the first. The second went to the man with shaking hands; the woman beside him carried it for him after Esme amended the form. A school cook and a watch repairer took the others.

I knew all four households. That did not mean I knew what each would do if the planks shifted.

At seven thirty-five, one Focus returned. Four of eight. I stood. My legs had stiffened, and the knot around my palm was soaked through.

“You can change your mind,” Esme said.

“Can I get the two silver back?”

“No.”

“Then let's get some use out of them.”

The ferryman helped her toward the gangway. She made him stop so she could see each bundle cross. They all reached the boat. She followed with her left foot held clear and his arm around her waist.

I tested the bearing's sling. Its weight label included the sling; I asked the kitchen man twice. Thirty-nine kilos meant only one kilo of margin. I removed a loose spanner someone had tucked into the webbing and left it on the empty case.

At seven thirty-six, I spent three Focus. One remained.

The bearing came up. I held the sling in my right hand, then steadied the ring with my left forearm. The skill needed touch, not a clenched fist. My cut hand could stay open.

On the first plank, the ring tried to roll in its sling. Weightlessness hadn't made it obedient. I stopped and turned it until its plane lay along the gangway. The ferry shifted at its ropes. I waited for the planks to settle, spending seconds I could not recover by wishing.

“Eight,” the ferryman called.

I moved.

At eleven seconds, water struck my shin. At fourteen, I stepped onto the second plank. I didn't look toward the empty case or the spanner. At eighteen, a deckhand took my elbow, and I put the bearing into its receiving cradle.

At twenty, the sling tightened under its full weight. The cradle held. I took my hand away.

The kitchen man came over behind me carrying the spanner. He put it in the cradle and nodded. He had no silver to offer. His patch was coming unstitched, and I found I didn't want him to explain that to me.

By seven thirty-nine, Esme had collected all four bundles. Twenty-six kilos of books lay under a passenger bench. The eight-kilo case remained ashore. So did my broken cart, somewhere beyond the warehouse.

“I'll write that the contents arrived,” she said.

“Will that buy a wheel?”

“No.”

I leaned against the rail. One Focus, no silver, a cut hand, and nine silver still owed on the cart. Economy would have left me more lifts. Long Carry had got us here. I would have to live with both facts.

The ferry pulled out at seven forty. Esme opened the housing book to find Dad's room. Her finger stopped.

She turned the page toward me. Across the entry ran a red stamp: VACATED BY CONSENT.

The date was yesterday. Yesterday I had helped him into that room, set coal beside the stove, and shown him how to latch the door.

Under the stamp, somebody had signed his name with a steady hand.
