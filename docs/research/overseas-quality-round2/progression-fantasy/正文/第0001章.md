# Chapter 1: The Weight Between Her Hands

The practice frame struck the bench on the second tick.

One corner opened. The little white stitch she had laid across the joint vanished, leaving her thumb on two pieces of ordinary wood.

Suri caught the frame before it slid into the shavings. She did it without looking up from the clock.

“Two,” she said.

“I know.”

Mara wished she had let the number stand. She had asked Suri to assess her; there was no sense snapping at the assessment. Outside the open workshop doors, the sorting crew tipped another load of salvage onto the cobbles. She could have been there, earning sixpence for the remaining afternoon.

Instead she was here, failing at something she could do with two scraps and no weight on them.

Suri laid the frame flat. It was a square of pine with three pegged corners and one loose mortise joint. A small canvas bag hung from its lower rail. The bag contained a single brick.

“Tell me what happened.”

“The stitch broke.”

“Before that.”

Mara rubbed her thumb against her apron. “I took the clamp off.”

“Before that.”

She stared at the joint. Her hand still remembered the small sideways kick of the rail. “I let it move.”

Suri set the clamp beside the frame. Its wide jaw had covered the hole where the wooden peg needed to go. That was the point of the exercise: a stitch would hold the corner while Mara removed the clamp and drove the peg. Otherwise anyone could have done the work with a mallet and a second pair of hands.

“You tried to catch it after it moved,” Suri said. “A stitch keeps a fit. It doesn't make one.”

Mara knew that. Every child with the knack heard it before anyone let them touch good timber. Bare skin, dry wood, two surfaces already touching. One joint at a time, no farther across than the span of your fingers. Six ticks, if it took properly. It could not pull a gap shut or mend a broken fiber.

Knowing had not helped her feel when the fit began to go.

In her apron pocket, her mother's bond notice scratched against her wrist. It was Thursday afternoon. Nine shillings by Saturday noon to keep their baking stall for the winter. Five shillings sat in the biscuit tin at home. A supervised repair paid four, materials included. Passing this exercise would let Mara take one.

Suri glanced toward the sorting crew. “You can still catch most of the shift.”

Mara pictured coming home with sixpence. Her mother would count it carefully and thank her. They would still have a long way to go.

“Put the brick back on,” she said.

Suri pulled up a stool. “Then stop watching the clock.”

She reset the corner and tightened the clamp. Mara pinched the joint between her thumb and first two fingers. Beneath the rough surface lay a faint answering sensation, like running a wet finger around a cup's rim, except that it had no sound. She could find that response in dry wood. Producing the stitch meant keeping her attention on it until a white thread appeared.

She pressed harder.

“What changed?” Suri asked.

“Nothing.”

“Then why are you squeezing?”

Mara eased her grip. The response became clearer. She had been crushing her fingertips against the grain until she could feel only her own pulse.

“Try opening the clamp a fraction. No stitch yet.”

The screw turned. Under Mara's fingers the response flickered, then divided into two uneven tremors. The rail wanted to sag and twist at once.

She tightened the clamp again. The tremors settled together.

“There,” she said.

Suri nodded. “Now you have something to notice.”

For several minutes Mara turned the screw back and forth without using the knack. At first she could identify the change only after the rail had visibly shifted. Then she caught it while the chalk lines across the corner still met. A little pressure upward under the rail brought the single response back.

Holding the weight with her left hand, she laid a stitch with her right.

White light seeped between her fingers. She removed the clamp and let her left hand fall away.

The stitch lasted three ticks.

This time Suri let the frame drop onto a folded sack.

Mara swore. Then she pulled the frame toward her before Suri could ask.

The chalk lines had shifted sideways. She had lifted the rail above its proper place while trying to support the brick. Her stitch had held that crooked fit until the hanging weight dragged the corner apart.

“I need a third hand.”

“Would be popular.” Suri looked at the offcut bin.

Mara fetched a wedge and pushed it under the lower rail. With its weight supported, the corner seated squarely. She checked the chalk, loosened the clamp, checked again. The response beneath her fingers stayed whole.

On her third attempt, the stitch took cleanly. She slid the clamp away, reached for the mallet, and knocked the wedge out with its handle.

Four ticks. The joint opened with a dry click.

“Too fast,” Mara said before Suri spoke.

Her thumb had begun to ache with cold. She tucked it briefly into her armpit. Staying wood took warmth, though the white thread gave off none. She had once tried to keep two broken crate slats together and ended up with a numb hand and two broken crate slats. Her mother had made her eat supper left-handed.

“One more before you warm up,” Suri said. “Four attempts is the bench limit. If you lose feeling sooner, you stop sooner.”

Mara reset the frame. This time she drew a line along the wedge where it met the rail.

“What is that for?”

“So I can see how much I'm moving it.”

She formed the stitch while the wedge still carried the brick. Then she eased the wedge backward, a sliver at a time. Through her fingers she felt the joined response tighten. She stopped moving. It steadied. Another sliver, another pause.

The wedge came free on the fourth tick.

She reached for the peg. The stitch disappeared on the sixth, just as her mallet struck. The rail sagged, trapping the peg halfway in.

For a moment Mara could only look at it.

Then Suri took the mallet from her cold hand.

“Six,” she said.

“I didn't finish.”

“You transferred the weight. Now we need a sequence you can finish.”

Mara wanted another try so badly that she had already reached for the clamp when she noticed she could not feel its screw against her thumb.

Suri pushed a wrapped warming stone across the bench. “Twenty minutes. All the feeling back before you touch the frame.”

“I'll lose the sequence.”

“Write it down.”

Mara took a pencil from behind her ear with her left hand. On the back of the bond notice she wrote: *Seat. Support. Stitch. Ease. Peg.* Underneath she added: *Put the mallet where the hand will be.*

Suri returned to her accounts. Mara held the stone through its cloth and watched the sorting crew wheel away their last full barrow. The sixpence was gone now. She wondered whether her mother would ask what the morning's practice had cost, and whether she would have the courage to count the afternoon as part of it.

When the clock's minute hand had moved twenty marks, Suri made her close her eyes and identify which fingertip touched the blunt end of the pencil. Mara got all five right. The deep ache had gone.

They reset the frame once more.

Mara placed the peg and mallet beside her left hand. She worked the wedge out to her pencil mark before making the stitch; beyond that point, the response began to divide, so she stopped there. Less distance to travel afterward.

The thread appeared. She took off the clamp, eased the remaining wedge free, and drove the peg on the fifth tick.

The sixth passed.

The brick stayed above the sack.

She lifted her right hand. A pale stripe marked the pad of her thumb, but she could feel the grain when she touched it again. The frame held because the peg held. Her stitch had already gone.

Suri handed her a repair docket.

“Seed Exchange. Cart tailboard. Eight tomorrow. Four shillings for a sound job, less any new stock you use. I'll supervise.”

Mara read it twice. “Is the joint like this one?”

“I haven't seen it yet.”

“What if I can't do it?”

“Then we find out before anybody loads the cart.”

Mara folded the docket around the bond notice. At the doorway she turned back, picked up the wedge, and asked whether she could borrow it.

“Put it on the tool sheet,” Suri said.

Mara did, carefully writing her name beneath the list of things she would have to return.
