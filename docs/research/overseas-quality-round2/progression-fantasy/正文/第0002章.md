# Chapter 2: A Shilling of Dry Wood

The tailboard lay on two flour sacks behind the Seed Exchange, with a dark stain across its bottom half. Mara put down her tool roll and hoped the stain was old.

It was five minutes to eight on Friday. Across the quay, porters were already loading the morning ferry. The exchange cart had to join its queue by nine.

Jo, the driver, crouched beside her. “Lower right corner. I can lash it for the trip if you can't fix it.”

He had a coil of rope ready. Mara appreciated that more than she would have appreciated confidence.

Suri leaned her tool case against the wall. “Your inspection,” she told Mara.

Yesterday's wedge was in Mara's apron. She touched it before turning the tailboard over.

Four rails framed a row of thin boards. At the lower right, the peg had worked loose. The joint opened when she pressed it. The tongue looked whole, and the outside of the upright felt dry.

She supported the lower rail with her wedge and brought the corner together. The frame rocked on the sacks. She moved a folded corner of sack beneath it until it settled.

Seat. Support.

She pinched the joint. The response under her fingers broke into a rough flutter. She shifted her grip, then squeezed harder.

Her own pulse drowned it out.

Mara released the wood. She had not made a stitch, but her cheeks felt hot with the familiar mistake.

“I can't get a clean fit,” she said.

“What would you need to see?” Suri asked.

The rest of the joint. Mara had been hoping to finish the repair without taking it apart.

She worked out the loose peg and eased the upright away. A sliver lifted with it. The inside cheek had split along the peg hole; damp darkened the fresh wood beneath. There was no sound surface there for a stitch to hold.

Jo ran a finger along the stain. “One sack burst yesterday. I washed the seed dust off.”

“When did the corner first open?”

“Before that.”

So she could not blame the washing for the split. She set the damaged upright aside.

“New upright,” she said. “Do you have a spare?”

“The parts stall does.” Jo pointed across the yard. “A shilling.”

Mara looked at Suri. The repair docket allowed four shillings altogether. She had understood the words *materials included* yesterday. They had seemed a small condition beside the prospect of being paid.

“It comes out of the four?”

“Yes,” Suri said. “You can decline before you cut anything.”

Five shillings at home. Three from this would make eight. Her mother might earn the last shilling from Friday's baking, if they sold everything and the flour bill was what the miller had promised.

Jo picked up his rope. “I've done three trips with it tied. Another won't kill it.”

Mara could leave him to the rope and take another job. She looked toward the workshop street. There might be another repair today. She had no idea what it would pay or whether Suri would give it to her.

“Get the upright,” she said. “Please. I'll prepare the frame.”

Jo went with her signed stock ticket. Mara arranged the peg punch and mallet within reach, just as she had at the bench.

The lower rail still would not seat squarely against the practice block she used to check its angle.

She pushed the frame flat. It sprang crooked when she let go.

“Something else is pulling it.”

Suri waited.

Mara inspected the three pegged corners. None had opened. She pressed each thin panel board in turn. Most shifted a little in their grooves. The lowest one stuck fast.

With the upright removed, she could see its swollen end wedged against the bottom of its slot. She drew it out. The frame relaxed against her checking block.

She had been about to stitch a new corner while a wet board forced the whole tailboard sideways.

“Can I shave the end?” she asked.

Suri picked up the board and examined its edge. “Show me how much.”

Mara marked a narrow line. Suri moved the pencil halfway back toward the end.

“Start there. You can take another shaving.”

By the time Jo returned, the panel moved in its groove. Mara had stopped twice to test it. The second shaving had been scarcely more than dust.

The new upright was standard cart stock, already grooved, but longer than the old one. Jo held its waste end while she sawed to her mark. The blade caught once. She backed it out, changed the angle, and finished without splitting the edge.

Mara inspected the exposed rail tongues before fitting them into the new sockets. Both were dry; the water had collected inside the split upright and along the panel's lower edge.

The clock above the exchange door showed half past eight.

“Still time to tie it,” Jo said.

Mara laid the new piece against the rails. Both tongues entered their sockets. Two loose joints now, with the panel boards wanting to spill between them.

Yesterday she had needed one hand for the mallet and one for the stitch. Today she seemed to need hands for the far corner, the boards, the wedge, and the frame itself.

She reached for the lower joint, then stopped.

One joint at a time. She could not make the whole side stay because she needed it to.

“Jo, have you got a straight piece of packing timber?”

He pulled a slat from the cart bed.

Mara laid it diagonally across the frame. With small wedges beneath its ends, it kept the tailboard square. Suri passed her a clamp when she reached into the tool case. She clamped the upper corner, leaving the lower one free for the stitch.

The damaged upright lay beside her knee. She kept it there. Looking at the split reminded her to support the new wood before she asked it to carry anything.

The response at the lower joint settled beneath her fingers.

“Watch the clock,” she told Jo.

The stitch appeared. She eased out the supporting wedge, felt the response tighten, and paused. On the next movement the load settled. She drove the peg on Jo's fifth count.

The white thread faded.

One corner held. The upper corner still needed its clamp.

Mara flexed her right hand. The thumb was cold, but she could feel the little ridges of the peg head. One attempt. She could do another if the feeling stayed.

She moved the clamp to the middle of the upright, supporting the wood while she prepared the upper joint. The same sequence ought to work.

But her right wrist hit the diagonal slat when she tried the mallet movement.

She tested the movement again without a stitch. No room.

Jo watched her reposition the tools. “Would it help if I held that?”

“The mallet?”

“Unless it's a secret.”

She laughed, surprised into it. “No. It's just the bit I finally learned.”

Mara kept her fingers on the upper joint. Jo took the mallet. They rehearsed once: she would release the clamp, ease the wedge, and say when the weight had settled; he would drive the waiting peg.

On the real attempt he struck on the fourth count. One blow left the peg proud. Mara felt the fit hold through the second blow.

Then six passed, and she took her hand away.

Both corners remained shut.

Her thumb tingled as she rolled up her tools. She tucked it against her palm while they tested the work. She wanted payment for a sound repair, and the pegs had to earn it.

They raised and lowered the empty tailboard on its hinges. Suri inspected the new joints while Jo loaded the cart. The sacks rested on the bed, clear of the repaired board. With the tailboard shut and latched, he rolled the cart over a low packing slat. Mara walked beside the repaired corner, watching the chalk lines she had drawn across it.

They still met when the wheels came down.

Suri checked the pegs again and signed the docket. Jo handed Mara three shillings and tucked the stock receipt into his book.

The exchange clock showed ten minutes to nine.

“You can keep the rope ready,” Mara told him.

“Always do.” He looped it over the cart handle and took his place behind a barrow of onions.

Mara sat on the empty flour sacks with the wrapped stone between her hands. Three coins lay in her lap. She put them in the inner pocket of her apron, apart from the borrowed wedge.

Suri picked up the broken upright. “Back to the shop?”

“Could I bring that?”

“What for?”

“I want to know if there's a way to find the split before opening the joint.”

“Perhaps. Some defects give a response. Some don't.”

Mara took the wood when Suri offered it. She would have to learn which was which. For now, she could see the split because she had taken the corner apart, and she still needed the stone to warm her hand.
