# Chapter 2: The Right to Let Go

At twenty past three, Mara put four red tiles on the laundry counter and moved the hospital receipt beside them.

"At two, I could have started the four-hour wash," she told Enna. "It would have ended at six, and fifteen hours on the winter racks would have left the sheets dry by nine tomorrow, one hour before the ten o'clock collection. But the repair needs these same tiles to hold the branch cold while we save the spindle."

"If we restore the line before five, the regular allotment gives us four hours of washing from five to nine. The last batch then needs fifteen hours on the racks. That's noon tomorrow, two hours after collection."

"How late?"

"Two hours. Noon, if we work through the night."

Enna pushed the receipt back. "Tell the hospital now. Give them time to ask someone else."

Mara had been hoping to postpone that part until she could call it a success. Instead she wrote the message, offered to pay for replacement sheets, and sent it with the baker's girl.

She wanted to help Iven keep something he could carry into a hearing. She also wanted him to come back when there was no inspection left to do. Neither wish would warm a sheet. She checked the lost working hours again before lifting the tiles.

When she came out, Iven stood beside the cabinet with his case closed. Pell was fetching the branch registry; his request had named the storehouse and required her to enter the spindle as disputed evidence.

"They can transfer me for this," he said.

Mara settled the tiles in their carrying box. "Away from the canal?"

"Away from meters, probably. Complaints office."

"You would be unbearable in a complaints office."

"I suspect that's the point."

He smiled, but his hands stayed on the case. She wanted, unexpectedly, to put hers over them. Wanting was easier than deciding what the gesture would promise.

"The tiles cost me the order," she said. "I can still choose to spend them. You don't owe me anything personal because I do."

"I know."

"And if the spindle becomes the only bridge we can use?"

He looked at the laundry window. "Then ask me again. Don't melt it while I'm holding the collar."

That answer contained something she could work with. She had expected an assurance that he would sacrifice it, cheaply given before the moment arrived.

Instead he had kept the decision.

They had laid out the materials during the past hour: copper tubing from Mara's stock, two plain clamps, a saw, a cradle to support the private branch. The longer repair was possible because they would remove the branch whole, with its metering socket attached. It would leave a gap she could bridge with her tubing.

Possible, provided its second valve behaved.

Pell returned with the registry and a boy carrying the evidence box. The storehouse had been reassigned to a winter dining club six months ago. Its heat allowance still appeared as zero.

Iven read the entry twice.

"My supervisor signed the reassignment," he said.

"Will that change your report?" Mara asked.

"It changes who receives a copy."

He wrote the district auditor's name on the envelope, then handed Pell the spindle. She sealed it in the box and kept it beside her feet. Mara watched the transfer. Whatever happened at the cabinet, the spindle could no longer become a convenient piece of unrecorded scrap.

At four, they loaded all four tiles into the feeder. The collar's dial showed one hour for the heavier work. Tiles that could have run her small kettle all evening would be dead by five.

Mara's throat tightened. She checked the drain again.

"Same limits?" Iven asked.

"The isolation needs more pressure. Two divisions higher. Either of us releases if the needle reaches the red line."

"Even if we're nearly finished."

She looked at him. "Even then."

Pell witnessed the new settings. They spoke their consent, inserted the keys, and brought the collar up slowly. Mara felt the branch settle against the pressure of their paired hold.

The collar had no preference for lovers, spouses, or strangers. It demanded two willing people because one frightened worker could otherwise be ordered to endure a failing line alone. She had explained that to apprentices. Today she understood why the explanation had always sounded insufficient.

"I'm moving my left foot," Iven said.

"You can stop asking so solemnly. Just warn me."

"Left foot," he repeated, with a gravity that made her laugh.

It helped. She found room to breathe over the saw.

For twenty minutes, the work went well. Iven supported the branch in its cradle while she cut the downstream joint. Pell called the dial readings. People gathered at a respectful distance, then lost patience with respect and began offering advice.

Mara asked Pell to send them back behind the pump.

The second joint resisted. A brown crust covered the seam where the private pipe entered the cabinet.

"Old solder," Mara said. "I need to score through it."

She shifted the saw. The branch jerked in its cradle.

Iven caught it, keeping his key straight. The needle jumped two divisions.

"Stop cutting," Pell said.

Mara stopped. The pressure held just below red. Beyond the cabinet, under the paving, something knocked twice.

The unseen valve had opened farther.

"Someone at the storehouse is drawing," Iven said.

Mara could not know whether it was deliberate. At this distance it made no difference. The isolation was holding back a stronger pull than their estimate allowed.

She found the emergency pinch beneath her thumb. Closing it would flatten the soft private pipe, reduce the pressure, and ruin the metering socket they wanted to preserve. With the spindle removed, the socket's wear marks were their remaining physical link to the branch.

"I can pinch it," she said. "We'll lose the socket."

"Do it."

The speed of his answer startled her.

"The red line," he said. "We agreed."

It had not reached red yet. She had time for one more cut. She could almost feel the saw finding the last thin strip of copper, everything saved if she held steady enough.

Her thumb stayed on the pinch.

Then the needle moved.

"Release," she said.

Iven withdrew his key with hers. Heat rushed into the stone bed. A gust of hot air rose harmlessly through its vents, and the private pipe sagged into the cradle.

For a moment Mara heard only her own breathing.

They had kept their promise. They had also spent the heat they needed to finish.

Pell checked the feeder. "Seventeen minutes remain in the tiles."

The safe drain had consumed part of their reserve. They had twenty-two minutes until five.

Mara pressed her palms against her coat. They wanted to shake.

"We pinch it before we restart," she said. "Then finish the cut. The bridge is already shaped."

Iven crouched to inspect the socket. "Take a rubbing first? A minute. We have the spindle and the registry. We can show what we destroyed."

"A minute," she agreed.

He held paper against the socket while she rubbed it with the side of her carpenter's pencil. Pell signed the rubbing and recorded the emergency damage. It would give an auditor a question to ask. It would not give Iven the intact proof he had wanted.

Mara closed the pinch. Copper folded with an ugly squeal.

They reset their keys. Her voice shook during the consent. Iven waited until she finished before speaking his own.

This time the pressure stayed low. She cut through, lifted the damaged branch into its cradle, and fitted her copper bridge. The second clamp slipped from her cold fingers. Iven caught it against his boot and nudged it within reach.

He did not tell her to hurry.

Pell counted the remaining minutes anyway.

At twelve minutes to five, Mara tightened the last clamp. At nine minutes to five, Pell opened the public feed a quarter turn. The bridge held. She opened it halfway, then fully before five, checking each joint with paper that would brown if heat escaped.

The paper stayed white. They withdrew their keys and drained the remaining purchased heat into the stone.

The furnace's regular evening allotment now had a clear path through the repaired street. It would run until nine; her tiles had held the dangerous isolation, not supplied the laundry's wash water.

Inside the laundry, the kettle knocked. Enna leaned out of the door and raised both arms, then disappeared inside to open the taps.

Mara sat down on the paving. Her empty tile box lay beside her.

Iven sat a little distance away. He looked exhausted and distinctly pleased, until Pell handed him the evidence receipt.

"There will be a hearing," she said. "That is all I can promise."

"Put me down as a witness," Mara said.

After Pell left, Iven turned the receipt over in his fingers.

"I wanted the whole thing," he said.

"I wanted the hospital order."

Neither loss improved because they had named it. She was glad he had not tried to improve hers with gratitude.

From the shop came the smell of wet linen warming. She had a long night ahead, and a message from the hospital still to receive.

"I'll eat at eight," she said. "Ten minutes by the kettle. You can join me."

He looked up. "Would you like that?"

Mara rubbed the old groove on her thumb. Today had not made it disappear.

"Yes."

"Then I'd like to come."

She stood and offered him her hand. He took it to get up, then let go when his feet were under him.

At eight, she thought, she might offer it again.
