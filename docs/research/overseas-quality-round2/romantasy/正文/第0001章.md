# Chapter 1: The Unspent Tile

The hospital sheets were still gray when the kettle went cold.

Mara held her wrist above the steam pipe. Nothing. Beneath the floor, the feeder clicked three times, trying to draw heat that was no longer there.

At the far tub, Enna lowered a sheet into its own dirty water. "Please tell me that was the kettle."

"The whole street."

Outside, someone struck the public feeder with a shovel. That settled it. A bad kettle inconvenienced one business; a cold street brought out the shovels.

Mara opened the reserve cupboard. Five red clay tiles lay on their felt shelf. Each held enough heat for one hour of washing. The hospital cart collected at ten tomorrow; four hours of washing still stood between Mara and a finished order. The fifth was meant for drying the little room where Enna's boys slept.

She left them there and took her fitting tools.

Iven was already kneeling beside the street cabinet, his survey case balanced on a sack of turnips. He looked up as she approached. There was a pale crescent on his cheek where his scarf had failed to keep out the cold.

"Don't say it's interesting," she told him.

His mouth moved. "I was considering 'expensive.'"

For eight Thursdays he had brought his inspection sheets to her back table, where the kettle kept the ink fluid. He always paid for the tea. Last week he had mended Enna's stool while waiting for a meter to settle, then carefully put the borrowed tools back in the wrong drawer.

Mara had begun leaving the correct drawer open.

Today his case contained a small brass spindle with a numbered lead seal.

"Found that?" she asked.

"In the return pipe. It measures what passed through a private branch."

Behind him, the cabinet's red needle rested at zero. A thinner needle, the one recording the highest overnight flow, stood almost against its stop.

Someone had drawn heavily before the public feeder failed.

"Can you tell where it went?"

"With the spindle intact, and the address ledger. My office has the ledger."

He closed his hand around the brass. Mara noticed the protective movement before she noticed that he was shivering.

"My brother's repair shed lost its feed twice last month," he said. "The board blamed unpaid bills. He had the receipts."

That explained the extra Thursday visits to the canal district. Mara had imagined other reasons for some of them. She put her tools down more sharply than necessary.

Clerk Pell arrived from the ward office carrying a lock and a paper packet. She wore her official chain over a patched wool coat.

"The fracture is under the cabinet," she said. "At five, my emergency repair authorization expires. If we finish, the furnace's regular evening allotment still runs to nine. It just can't pass this break until I certify the bridge."

Mara looked toward her shop. Through the glass, Enna was wringing a sheet by hand.

"And if we miss five?"

"I seal the branch. A new repair request, a new place in the queue."

Pell sounded tired of the answer herself.

The cabinet rested on stone feet. Mara lay on the paving and slid a mirror underneath. The split crossed a copper elbow. Beside it ran an unauthorized branch, narrow enough to overlook beneath the accumulated dirt.

"I can bridge the elbow," she said. "But someone has to hold the branch cold while I work. A paired collar would do it."

Pell opened her packet. "A household covenant authorizes emergency draw."

Mara sat up.

Iven took the offered sheet, read three lines, and handed it back. "No."

He had answered before Mara found the clause. Liability continued after the repair. Either signatory's future heat could be seized for the other's excess.

Her former business partner had forged her name on just such a covenant. The board had taken her first shop's stove before it agreed to investigate the signature. Two winters later, her petition still occupied a shelf somewhere.

Pell knew. Her eyes dropped to Mara's bare left thumb, where the old registration ring had rubbed a permanent groove.

"The household form is the only one that draws from the public reserve," she said.

"Then we don't draw from it," Iven said. "A temporary work pair can use purchased heat."

"Purchased heat doesn't grow on turnips."

"Neither do households."

Mara nearly smiled. She was too busy resenting the relief of hearing him refuse.

Pell searched her packet again and found a narrow gray form. "You set a separate limit on each key. Both keys must stay in the collar. Either person can withdraw. Withdrawal drains the working heat into the stone bed. No one gets to hold another person's hand there."

"And no debt afterward," Mara said.

"None beyond the tiles you feed it. But the collar cannot make heat. You'll need your own."

Mara stood. "I have five."

Iven glanced toward the laundry. "How many can you spare?"

"One for a test."

It was the truth, although it left out the boys' room.

They fetched the collar from the ward office. Pell made Mara demonstrate that the stone bed beneath the cabinet was clear; a blocked drain could turn a safe release into a scalding one. Iven brushed it out while Mara examined the collar's two key sockets.

He worked without his gloves. The knuckle nearest his thumb was split.

She offered him the small tin from her tool roll.

"For that," she said, when he looked uncertain. "It catches copper dust."

"Thank you."

He used a little salve and passed it back. No attempt to touch her. Somehow that made the space between them more noticeable.

By the church clock it was two. They had three hours.

The four wash tiles could still meet the hospital's ten o'clock collection: starting at two, four hours at the small boiler would finish at six, then fifteen hours on the winter racks meant dry sheets by nine tomorrow. But once spent on laundry, they could not hold the branch cold for the repair.

Mara brought the fifth tile from the cupboard and told Enna where it was going. Enna said nothing until Mara reached the door.

"I'll bring the boys downstairs tonight. It holds warmth longer."

There went the part Mara had not said.

At the cabinet, she set her key to a low limit. Iven set his to match. Pell watched each turn, then pressed her clerk's stamp into the collars of wax protecting the settings.

The work pair required spoken consent, specific to the task. Mara had installed enough collars to know the words. She still had to swallow before she spoke them.

"I agree to hold this branch cold for the test. I can stop."

"I agree to hold it with you," Iven said. "I can stop."

Their keys warmed. Mara laid the tile in the feeder tray. Its red glaze darkened as the collar consumed its heat to hold the branch's flow still. The copper straightened with a faint, reluctant sound.

She could feel his key through hers: a second steady pressure, separate from her own. That was all the collar conveyed. It could not report honesty, fondness, or what he intended to do tomorrow.

It would have been convenient if it could.

Mara slid her probe into the fracture. The reading rose, steadied, then fell.

"The branch is pulling against us," she said. "There must be another valve upstream."

Iven reached for his case.

"Wait."

He stopped immediately, hand suspended above the latch.

She had spoken more sharply than she meant. A person shifting position could lever a key out. The collar would drain safely, but they would lose the test before she had the depth measurement.

"Tell me before you move."

"All right. May I reach down?"

"Now, yes."

He took out a folded plan one-handed. She leaned close enough to read it. The wool at his shoulder smelled of rain and the bitter soap her shop sold in winter.

On the plan, the branch ended at an empty municipal storehouse.

"Empty?" she asked.

"According to the board."

Mara knew that address. A cart delivered oranges there every Wednesday. The porter had asked her to wash six dining cloths and pay herself in fruit.

She told Iven. His jaw tightened. Then he asked for the probe reading, and wrote it down before asking anything else.

Fifteen minutes later, the tile lost its last color. Their keys cooled.

Mara inspected the exposed elbow. A length of clean brass would make a quick bridge. The spindle in Iven's case was exactly the right diameter; once melted and drawn, it would be enough.

She looked at the case. He followed her gaze.

"That is why you covered it," she said.

"I hoped there was another way."

"There is. Four tiles to hold a longer isolation while I fit copper from the shop. Then we have to cut the private branch out in one piece."

"Your washing heat."

Behind her window, the hospital sheets hung wet and gray. She wanted to be furious with him for having something worth protecting.

"I won't promise them yet," she said.

He drew his hand away from the case.

"Then don't. Let's find out exactly what it will cost."
